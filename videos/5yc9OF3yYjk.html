AFSD - LARGE METAL 3D PRINTING!

@octopusinc_ · 2026-08-21 · Hardware

This video explores Additive Friction Stir Deposition (AFSD), a solid-state metal 3D printing technology developed at Virginia Tech. Unlike traditional methods that melt metal, AFSD uses friction and pressure to bond material, creating fully dense parts with properties similar to forged metals. The process can utilize various feedstocks, including scrap metal, to produce "smart metal" components with embedded sensors.

This process creates fully dense, metallurgically bonded parts with properties similar to forged metals, avoiding porosity issues common in melt-based methods. The machine used is a large CNC machine with a powerful spindle motor and a linear actuator capable of imparting up to 7,000 lbs of downforce and 350 lb-ft of torque. The process involves feeding a solid bar of material (e.g., 0.5" x 2" aluminum) through a keyed, rotating tool that generates heat through shear deformation, allowing the material to flow like a liquid without melting. The control panel shows real-time data for spindle speed (e.g., 350 rpm), torque (e.g., 140 ft-lbs), actuator force (e.g., 1,700 lbs), and temperature (e.g., 330C for flow). The technology can print various ductile materials, including aluminum (e.g., 6061, 7075, 7050), steels, nickel alloys, titanium, and copper, provided the tooling material has a higher melting point. A significant advantage is its ability to use scrap metal (e.g., 80% dense twitch from automotive scrap, circuit boards) as feedstock, upcycling it into 100% dense components with mechanical performance up to 60% of forged 6061 T6. The process is energy-efficient as it doesn't require melting. AFSD also enables "smart metal" applications by embedding electronic sensors within metal components, printing over them to restore performance and protect the sensors. Examples include an aileron lift bracket with embedded temperature and strain sensors for structural health monitoring, and an aluminum video game controller with an embedded accelerometer. The technology is described as near-net shape, requiring post-machining for critical features, similar to other high-performance manufacturing processes. The largest known AFSD machine has a print bed volume of 30 ft x 20 ft x 12 ft.
