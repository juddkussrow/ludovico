It details how ATES works, its efficiency advantages over conventional systems, and why it has been widely adopted in the Netherlands but not in the US, despite early US Department of Energy research. The system works by pumping groundwater through a heat exchanger; in summer, heat is stored in one part of the aquifer, and in winter, that warm water is retrieved for heating while cold water is stored. ATES is presented as significantly more efficient than conventional systems: roughly five times more efficient for cooling than conventional geothermal and up to ten times more efficient than air source heat pumps for cooling, largely due to "free cooling" where stored cold water is simply pumped through a heat exchanger. Heating is up to four times more efficient than a gas furnace, with emission savings reaching 75%. The system typically pays for itself in two to ten years. The US Department of Energy proved its viability decades ago, but it has not been widely adopted in the US. In contrast, the Netherlands has over 3,000 ATES projects, accounting for 85% of global projects, driven by higher energy prices and strict government regulations on greenhouse gas emissions and building energy performance standards, which began in the early 1990s and required buildings to be energy neutral by 2022. The Dutch also developed quality standards for ATES design, installation, and operation, making contractors responsible for maintenance. Drawbacks of ATES include potential well corrosion, obstruction by mud/silt, and bacterial growth forming biofilms. For ATES to work, an aquifer and a climate with hot summers and cold winters are needed; 27% of global land is suitable, including broad swaths of North America. A study found 1,800 potential buildings in NYC boroughs could benefit, and ATES could reduce US natural gas and electricity consumption by 40%. However, in the US, ATES is currently expensive and unfamiliar, with one Minneapolis project opting for a different approach due to cost. A Chicago study found ATES 15-20% more expensive than existing storage but predicted it would break even after a few years of development.
