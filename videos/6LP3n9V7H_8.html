We Actually Have a Third Eye (And It’s Not Metaphysical)

@octopusinc_ · 2026-05-05 · Bio Gen

This video discusses the evolution of vertebrate eyes, focusing on a recent study that explains why human eyes differ from other animals. It highlights the concept of a "third eye" (the pineal gland) in humans and other vertebrates, tracing its evolutionary history from an ancestral worm-like creature. The video explains how vertebrate eyes are a repurposed version of a central midline eye, leading to their "inverted" structure compared to invertebrate eyes like those of an octopus.

It begins by acknowledging the complexity of the eye and Darwin's initial struggle to explain its evolution. The video explains that complex eyes evolved independently multiple times across different animal groups, all sharing the Pax-6 gene for eye and brain development. The evolutionary path started with simple light-sensitive opsins, progressing to eye spots, then folded cups for directional sensitivity, and finally pinhole eyes and lenses. This process, from a simple patch to a complex eye with a lens, could take as little as 400,000 years. A key distinction is drawn between human/mammalian eyes and invertebrate eyes (like octopuses). Human eyes have an inverted retina with neurons in front of photoreceptors, creating a blind spot, and use ciliary muscles to change lens shape. Octopus eyes have an everted retina, no blind spot, and move the entire lens to focus. The video posits that vertebrate eyes evolved from an ancestral worm-like creature 600 million years ago that had both lateral and a central midline eye. Due to environmental changes, this ancestor became a stationary filter feeder, losing its lateral eyes but retaining the central eye to detect day/night cycles. When climate changed again, and the ancestors returned to swimming, the central midline eye repurposed itself, growing cups that moved to the sides of the head, forming the paired eyes of vertebrates. This explains the "inverted" structure of vertebrate eyes as an "evolutionary baggage." The video then discusses the human pineal gland, located in the middle of the brain, as the remnant of this ancestral central eye. While in humans it primarily produces melatonin and regulates sleep, in other vertebrates like lizards and frogs, it manifests as a visible "parietal eye" with a cornea, lens, and retina. The human pineal gland still contains pinealocytes, similar to retinal cells, suggesting its light-sensing origin. The Cambrian explosion and the "light switch hypothesis" are mentioned as drivers for the rapid evolution of diverse eyes, as vision turned animals into active hunters, leading to an evolutionary arms race.
