Traditionally, battery electrodes are made by mixing powders with a liquid to form a slurry, which is then coated onto current collectors and baked dry in energy-intensive ovens. This "wet" process is slow and expensive. Dry electrode coating eliminates the liquid, slurry, and ovens, leading to faster production times and energy savings, with industry estimates suggesting cost reductions as high as 25%. The main challenge with dry coating is making the electrode powders stick to the current collectors. This is overcome by using special polymers like PTFE (Teflon) as a binder. Maxwell Technologies (acquired by Tesla) developed a process where dry electrode powder and PTFE are blasted through a jet mill, causing the PTFE to stretch into fibers that bind the active particles. Fraunhofer, a German research institute, uses two rollers spinning at different speeds to achieve a similar effect. The resulting dry film is then pressed onto current collectors. Tesla has recently scaled this technology for production vehicles, particularly by solving the challenge of applying the dry process to cathodes, which are more reactive. The solution involved blending PTFE fibers with more chemically stable polymers like PVDF to create a barrier, preventing unwanted side reactions. This innovation not only makes batteries cheaper but also improves performance by eliminating binder migration, a problem in wet processes that limits electrode thickness and energy density. Dry processing also benefits solid-state batteries by avoiding moisture sensitivity issues.
