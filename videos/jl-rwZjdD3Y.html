These traditional systems are expensive ($10,000-$15,000+), require permits, licensed electricians, and often panel upgrades, and are not portable. Pila, in contrast, plugs into a standard 120-V outlet, requires no electrician or permits, and is designed to be portable. Each Pila unit offers 1.6 kWh of storage, 2,400 W of continuous power, and a 3,600 W surge for up to 30 seconds. Multiple Pila units can form a "battery mesh operating system," coordinating over a local network (Thread) and supporting up to 64 units in a house, with testing for over a thousand units in apartment buildings. The system integrates with rooftop solar (Tesla and Enphase currently), utility data, appliance metering, and smart home platforms like Home Assistant (with Alexa and Google Home coming soon). Pila offers three modes: "Maximize Backup" (battery stays 100% charged), "Reduce Energy Costs" (charges when electricity is cheap, discharges when expensive), and "Optimize Solar Usage" (charges with solar, discharges after sunset). It also features a storm mode for automatic backup. The hardware has a touchscreen for setup, Wi-Fi, Home Assistant pairing, and mode settings, and a mobile app provides data visualization. The presenter installed three Pila units (totaling 4.8 kWh) to fill a 5 kWh storage gap in his home that his Enphase system couldn't address due to code limits. He demonstrates units in his office (powering Mac Studio, speakers, lamps, providing 11.5 hours of backup), network closet (powering servers, switches, cameras, providing 5 hours of backup, offloading Enphase), and living room (powering TV, home theater, lamps, extending Enphase usage). The Pila system successfully eliminated the need for his Enphase system to pull from the grid in early morning hours. However, Pila is not a whole-home backup solution; one unit or even three units (4.8 kWh) cannot run an entire house during multi-day outages, nor can it handle high-power loads like Level 2 EV chargers or central HVAC. An expansion accessory will double capacity to 3.2 kWh per unit. Each Pila unit is priced at $1,499. The video highlights that current US electrical codes (e.g., UL 3700) are not yet adapted for bidirectional power flow from plug-in batteries, but 28 states plus DC are considering legislation. Germany is ahead in this regard. The presenter emphasizes buying the product for what it can do today (backup, load shifting) rather than future promises.
