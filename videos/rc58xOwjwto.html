Early experiments involved training bees to push a yellow ball into a hole for a sugar reward, with a plastic demonstrator bee initially showing the task. Control groups without training failed, while trained bees progressively solved the task faster. Further experiments explored social learning, with bees observing a demonstrator bee, a "ghost demonstrator" (magnet-moved ball), or simply seeing the end result (ball already in the center). Bees taught by another bee passed almost every time, ghost-demonstrator bees passed 80% of the time, and bees seeing only the end result passed about a third of the time, suggesting inference. Notably, bees often improved on demonstrations by choosing the closest ball to the goal, rather than the furthest one shown. A later experiment tested abstract reasoning by training bees to roll a ball matching the platform's color. Bees trained on blue/red platforms passed when tested on yellow (86% pass rate), but failed on blue (0% pass rate), possibly due to neophobia. The most complex experiment adapted Wolfgang Köhler's "boxing banana" experiment for bees, requiring them to push a Styrofoam ball under a high flower to reach sugar water. Bees were pre-trained to associate the blue flower with a reward and that the ball was movable. In a subsequent test with a barrier obstructing constant visual feedback, 75% of trained bees (out of 22) successfully rolled the ball to the flower, compared to 5% of untrained bees. This demonstrated spontaneous problem-solving and tool use in varied ways, published in Science in 2026.
