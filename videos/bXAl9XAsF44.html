It starts with diffusion models, which are the "engine" behind generation, converting noise into coherent images. These are large files, ranging from 6GB for older models to 10-40GB for modern ones. Next, LoRAs (low-rank adaptation) are introduced as small, lightweight models that modify diffusion model behavior, steering them towards specific styles or subjects not in the original training data, such as a specific person's face or an art style. LoRAs are widely used due to their small file size and ease of use. The video then details three other crucial model types: CLIP models, VAEs, and ControlNets. CLIP models (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) translate text prompts into numerical representations (conditioning) that the diffusion model can understand. Using an incompatible CLIP model can lead to misinterpretations or errors. VAEs (variational autoencoders) convert data between the latent space (AI's abstract representation) and the visible pixel space (RGB images). A good VAE ensures sharp images and correct colors, while a mismatched one can degrade output quality. ControlNets provide structural guidance to diffusion models, allowing users to control composition, pose, or outlines using reference images processed into depth maps, Canny edges, or Open Pose skeletons. This enables precise, repeatable results. The video also provides a brief history of diffusion models, starting with Stable Diffusion 1.5 (2023), which was groundbreaking but limited in quality, especially for hands and faces. SDXL (2024) significantly upgraded quality and capability. Newer models like Waifu, Kwan, Flux, Z Image, Gemini, and Nano Banana offer diverse strengths in speed, quality, or video generation, with varying licensing (open-source to closed-source).
