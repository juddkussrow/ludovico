The creator explains that the recent influx of UV printers is largely due to Epson making its printheads, specifically the i3200, available to manufacturers since 2019, rather than expiring patents. The HeyGears G1X uses the Epson i3200 printhead, which is superior to the Epson 1080 in its ability to push thicker, more viscous inks. HeyGears offers three versions of its machine, loaded with regular UV ink, DT ink (fabric ink), or real 3D ink with a thicker formula. The i3200 printhead is piezoelectric, using a crystal to push ink, and HeyGears enhances its performance by mounting a heater on the printhead. A key concern is the printhead replacement cost of $1,299 for the i3200, which is considered a consumable with an estimated lifespan of 2,100 hours and a 180-day warranty. This is significantly more expensive than a 1080 type printhead ($200-$300). Switching between ink types (UV 3D resin or DTF) is complicated, requiring cleaning and flushing lines, unlike the xTool Omni which has two printheads for different ink types. HeyGears uses an internal ink system with a separate tank, anticipating higher ink consumption for 3D models. They also use fiber optic cables for printhead connections due to frequent bending. The machine requires constant ink circulation (3-5ml in 8 hours, totaling 450ml/month or $300-$500/year idle) to prevent clogging, a maintenance challenge that Morpho reportedly addresses better with its ink design. The second problem HeyGears addresses is supporting the 3D shape, using water-soluble ink/resin for supports that dissolve to reveal the final object. Unlike resin vat printing, HeyGears' method requires full support for each layer. 3D prints can take 20-30 hours, plus 6-7 hours for support removal. The third problem is achieving full color in 3D. The G1X creates a colored shell around a white interior. It converts RGB to CYMK and uses dithering for color mixing. The challenge is applying this to a 3D object, converting colors to ink percentages, and dealing with non-opaque inks that build up layers in 3D space. HeyGears developed its own software and algorithms for this, as patents from major players like Stratasys are still active. The vertical resolution of the G1X is about half that of a standard photo. The final problem is keeping layers flat. HeyGears uses a proprietary self-leveling ink formula and a lidar sensor to check layers and calibrate subsequent passes, avoiding the traditional squeegee method that wastes ink.
