The video details how carbon, in its allotrope forms like graphite and graphene, can be highly conductive. The challenge lies in translating the 2D conductivity of graphene into a 3D wire structure, which is achieved by rolling graphene sheets into nanotubes. Production of carbon nanotubes is difficult and expensive, but advancements like "wet spinning" and a cleaning process called LAST (Lyotropic Liquid Crystal Assisted Surface Texturing) are improving conductivity. While this is still significantly lower than aluminum (35 megasiemens per meter) and copper (58 megasiemens per meter), the lighter weight of carbon nanotubes allows for more windings, potentially leading to lighter, more efficient, and cooler motors. The environmental benefits of reducing copper mining are also discussed, including the potential for carbon-negative production methods. The main hurdles to widespread adoption are cost (carbon nanotubes are 20-80 cents per gram compared to under 1 cent per gram for copper) and achieving higher conductivity. A Spanish team recently produced carbon nanotubes with 24.5 megasiemens per meter conductivity, approaching half that of copper, by adding aluminum trichloride.
